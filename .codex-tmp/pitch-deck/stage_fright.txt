Detail	on	each	ﬁnding
1.	AI	speech-coaching	app	—	25.2%	anxiety	reduction,
60.5%	competency	gain
“Addressing	Public	Speaking	Anxiety	with	an	AI	Speech	Coach”
—	Garcia	Jr.,	Pascual,	Sybingco	&	Ong,	De	La	Salle	University	Manila,
published	in	the	
Proceedings	of	the	32nd	International	Conference	on
Computers	in	Education
	
(2024).
20	Filipino	senior-high	students	(grouped	by	baseline	anxiety:	low,
moderate,	high)	used	the	AI	tool	
Yoodli
	
for	a	minimum	of	10
minutes	daily	over	one	week.
Anxiety	was	measured	with	the	validated	
Personal	Report	of
Public	Speaking	Anxiety	(PRPSA)
	
scale;	performance	was
measured	with	the	
Competent	Speaker	Speech	Evaluation
Form	(CSSEF)
.
Results	were	statistically	signiﬁcant	across	all	three	anxiety
groups	(p-values	ranging	from	0.03	to	<0.0001):

2.	Gamiﬁed	VR	exposure	therapy	—	large	eﬀect	size,
~28–31%	reliable	clinical	change
Four-armed	RCT
	
in	
Frontiers	in	Virtual	Reality
	
(2023)	testing
gamiﬁed	virtual	reality	exposure	therapy	(VRET)	against	online
psychoeducation	and	a	waitlist	in	100	adolescents	with	public
speaking	anxiety.
VRET	produced	a	large	within-group	eﬀect	size	(
d
	
=	0.83)	versus	a
smaller	eﬀect	for	online	psychoeducation	alone	(
d
	
=	0.33).
Between	VRET	and	waitlist	control,	the	diﬀerence	was	statistically
signiﬁcant	(
p
	
=	.015,	
d
	
=	0.61).
28–31%	of	participants	across	intervention	arms	met	criteria	for
“clinically	reliable	change.”
Zero	participants	got	worse	over	the	course	of	treatment.
3.	VR	audience	training	transfers	to	real-life
performance	—	
d
	
=	0.84	on	conﬁdence
“Public	speaking	training	in	front	of	a	supportive	audience	in
Virtual	Reality	improves	performance	in	real-life”
	
—	
Scientiﬁc
Reports
	
(Nature),	2023.
73	participants	trained	with	either	a	supportive,	unsupportive,	or
no	virtual	audience,	then	gave	a	real	presentation	(assessed	via
Zoom).

Those	trained	with	a	
supportive
	
virtual	audience	received
signiﬁcantly	higher	instructor-rated	conﬁdence	scores	(
d
	
=	0.84,	a
large	eﬀect)	than	those	trained	with	an	unsupportive	one.
General	self-eﬀicacy	improved	across	all	VR-trained	groups	(
d
	
=
0.30)	and	the	gain	held	up	in	the	real,	non-VR	presentation.
4.	VR-CBT	—	comparable	to	traditional	therapy,	with
better	retention
Anderson,	Zimand,	Hodges	&	Rothbaum,	published	RCT	(PubMed-
indexed).
88	participants	split	between	VR-based	CBT,	traditional	CBT,	and	a
waitlist	control.
Both	treatment	groups	signiﬁcantly	outperformed	the	waitlist	on	4
of	5	anxiety	measures.
Notably,	the	dropout	rate	for	traditional	CBT	was	roughly	double
that	of	VRCBT	—	i.e.,	people	were	more	likely	to	stick	with	the	VR-
based	treatment	through	completion.
5.	Meta-analysis	—	the	strongest	form	of	evidence
here
Reeves,	Curran,	Gleeson	&	Hanna	(2022),	
Behavior	Modiﬁcation
(SAGE)	—	a	meta-analysis	pooling	11	studies	and	508	total
participants	comparing	VR	exposure	therapy	and	in-person	(“in	vivo”)
exposure	therapy	against	control	conditions	for	public	speaking
anxiety.
VR	exposure	therapy	showed	a	large,	statistically	signiﬁcant	eﬀect
versus	control	(Hedges’	
g
	
=	−1.39,	
p
	
<	.001).
In-person	exposure	therapy	showed	a	very	similar	large	eﬀect	(
g
	
=
−1.41,	
p
	
<	.001)	—	meaning	VR-based	approaches	performed
about	as	well	as	traditional	in-person	exposure	therapy.
A	note	on	terminology
Most	of	the	rigorous,	peer-reviewed	evidence	base	here	is	on	
VR
exposure	therapy
	
for	public	speaking	anxiety,	some	of	which
incorporates	AI-driven	components	(responsive	virtual	audiences,
speech	analysis).	Dedicated	
AI	feedback	tools
	
(like	Yoodli,	Orai,
Poised)	are	a	newer	category	with	far	less	published	research	—	the
De	La	Salle	University	study	above	is	one	of	the	only	peer-reviewed
studies	currently	available	with	hard	percentage	ﬁgures,	so	treat	it	as
promising	early	evidence	rather	than	an	established	consensus	the
way	the	VR	meta-analysis	is.
