===== PAGE 2 =====
What the jury scores.
Five things, equally weighted. Everything today points at one of them.
01 Problem Who has this problem, and how do you know it is real?
02 Solution What did you build, and why this approach over the obvious one?
03 Demo It runs live, in front of the jury. Not a slide. Not a video.
04 Impact If a thousand people used this, what actually changes?
05 Team Everyone contributed, and anyone can explain any part of it.


===== PAGE 3 =====
Friday. Six minutes each.
Ten teams, one jury, one room. Here is exactly how your slot runs.
YOUR 4 MINUTES
Suggested split: 2 minutes pitch, 2 minutes live 
demo.
How you divide it is your call. Some teams demo first and 
explain after. That works too.
THEN 2 MINUTES OF QUESTIONS
The jury asks. Anyone on the team can answer.
This is where they find out whether you understand your 
own build.
Live from your laptop You plug in and run the real thing. No pre-recorded video.
The clock is hard At four minutes you stop, mid sentence if necessary. Ten teams, one hour.
Everyone speaks The jury scores the team, not the presenter. Silent members cost you.


===== PAGE 4 =====
Now the hard question.
You know what you are building. Can you say who it is for and why it matters?
WHAT LOSES ON FRIDAY
“We built an AI tool that helps with studying.”
Nobody in particular has this problem. Nothing in particular 
changes. The jury has no way to care.
WHAT WINS
“Grade 11 students revising alone at 11pm have 
nobody to ask. Three apps do flashcards. None 
of them answer a question about the thing in 
front of you.”
A real person, a real moment, and homework done. Now 
the jury leans in.
Same build. Same code. Completely different score.
The next 45 minutes are about getting your project from the left box to the right one.


===== PAGE 5 =====
The validation canvas. 45 minutes.
Two pages on your table. Work through them in order, they build on each other.
1 Who you serve
One specific person, not a category.
2 What is broken today
What they do instead, and why it fails them.
3 Your research
How common is it, and who already solves it?
4 Your promise
One sentence, no jargon.
5 Why AI, why now
Why this needs a model and not a spreadsheet.
6 The change you make
If a thousand people used it, what is different?
7 What you are not building
Three things you are deliberately cutting.
8 What must be true
The assumption that sinks you if it is wrong.


===== PAGE 6 =====
Two rules while you fill it in.
Otherwise this becomes a creative writing exercise, and the jury can always tell.
Be specific enough to be wrong
“Students” is not a person. “My sister, Grade 9, hates maths homework” is. Specific claims can be checked, and 
checked claims are the ones that survive the questions.
Point your AI at your own idea
You have spent two days pointing these tools at code. Now use one as a research assistant: how common is this 
problem, who already solves it, what do they charge, what do people complain about? Fifteen minutes of that changes 
what you build.
One catch: models invent statistics. No source named, no number used. The jury will ask.


===== PAGE 7 =====
What makes a good prototype.
Not a product. A prototype is an argument you can click.
One path, all the way through A single flow that works start to finish beats five half -finished features. The jury 
sees one thing work, not five things almost work.
Real enough to believe Plausible data, sensible outputs, a name. A demo full of “test test” reads as 
unfinished, even when the engineering underneath is good.
It survives a stranger Someone who has never seen it should get through the main flow without you 
narrating. T est that on another team before Friday.
It fails politely Things break on stage. A loading state, a friendly error, a fallback answer. Never a 
red stack trace on the projector.
You can explain every part AI wrote most of it. Fine, that is the week. But if you cannot say what a piece does, 
cut it or learn it before Friday.


===== PAGE 8 =====
Before you call it done.
Run this list on your own build. Anything you cannot tick is your next hour of work.
Someone outside your team completed the main flow without help.
It runs on the laptop you will actually plug in on Friday.
It works without your home wifi, on a network you have not tested.
You have a fallback if the model is slow or the API is down.
Nothing on screen says test, asdf, or lorem ipsum.
You can start it from cold in under thirty seconds.


===== PAGE 9 =====
What a good pitch looks like.
Two minutes of talking. Here is where every second goes.
15s Who you are T eam, track, what the thing is called, one line on what it does.
30s The person and the problem Your box 1 and box 3. Name a person, then a finding with a 
source.
25s What you built The promise, in plain words. Not the tech stack.
2 min Demo Stop talking about it and show it. This is the part they remember.
20s Why it matters Your box 6. What changes if this exists.
15s Team and next Who did what, and what you would build with another week.


===== PAGE 10 =====
Four ways teams lose the room.
All four are avoidable, and all four will happen tomorrow at the mock pitches.
The tour
“Here is the login, here is the settings page.” Nobody 
cares. Show the one moment that matters.
The tech list
Naming six models and four frameworks is not a pitch. 
What does it do for the person?
The apology
“It's not finished, sorry, this bit is broken.” Never 
apologise. Show what works, confidently.
The one voice
One person talks for four minutes while four teammates 
stand behind them. The jury notices.


===== PAGE 11 =====
What to prepare, starting now.
You have seen one team do it. Six slides, and none of them are hard if your canvas is honest.
FROM YOUR CANVAS, STRAIGHT INTO SLIDES
Box 1 and 2  →  the problem slide
Box 3  →  the line that proves it is real
Box 4  →  what you built
Box 6  →  why it matters
Box 8  →  the honest answer when the jury pushes
DECIDE THESE AS A TEAM TODAY
Who talks, and who drives the demo.
Which laptop plugs in, and does it have the 
adapter.
The exact clicks of your demo, in order.
What you show if the internet dies.
Nobody writes a pitch on Friday morning. Write it tonight, badly, and fix it tomorrow.


===== PAGE 12 =====
Tomorrow. Thursday.
Two rounds of feedback before the jury ever sees you. Use them.
FIRST HALF
Demo to each other,
in pairs.
Two teams, screens open, no slides.
You watch each other's build and give real feedback, the 
kind you would want.
Then you swap and do it again with another team.
AFTER THE BREAK
Mock pitches,
full room.
Every team pitches, on the clock, exactly as on Friday.
Every other team asks the presenting team a question.
You leave with a list of what to fix, written by nine teams 
who just watched you.
